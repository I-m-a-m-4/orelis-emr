Clinical readiness assessment

What a hospital EMR needs that Orelis doesn’t have yet

Ordered by triage, not by effort. The question a hospital’s medical director will ask is not “what features are missing” but “can this system hurt a patient, and can I get my records out of it”. Everything below is graded against those two questions first.

Assessed: firestore.rules · src/lib · src/app/dashboard · src/ai Not assessed: deployment, backups, contracts

What’s genuinely strong already

Worth stating plainly, because the list below is long and it would be easy to read it as a verdict on the whole system. Several things here are better than what mid-market EMR vendors ship.

Credit where it’s due

Genuine offline-first writes Two local stores, a documented non-blocking write contract, and a sync layer that refuses to stamp success it didn’t achieve. Most EMRs simply fail in a ward with no signal.
Multi-tenant from the ground up One clinicId boundary, consistently applied in every query, plus a cache-ownership guard that wipes a device when the tenant changes.
Audit logging with pre-delete snapshots Including access logging on chart reads, which is the event a privacy investigation actually asks for and which most systems never record.
A real AI gateway One route, role floors per flow, usage accounting, lazy flow imports. The right shape, not a pile of endpoints.
Server-side cascade deletes Chunked under the 500-write batch ceiling, Auth deleted only after Firestore succeeds. Someone thought about the half-finished case.
RBAC now proven, not just written 42 emulator tests covering cross-tenant isolation, privilege escalation and role floors. Run with npm run test:rules.

Can hurt a patient7 gaps

Unsafe to sell into a hospital

These are not feature gaps. Each one is a mechanism by which the software can contribute to a clinical error, or by which a record can stop being defensible.

Nothing checks a prescription against the patient’s allergies

Patient.allergies[] is captured and then never consulted. A doctor can prescribe penicillin to a patient whose chart records a penicillin allergy, and the system will not say a word. DrugSafetyChecker exists, but it’s a standalone panel a clinician has to remember to open — it is not in the prescribing path. Allergy checking has to be a blocking interruption at the moment of order, not a tool.

src/lib/types.ts → allergies[] src/components/dashboard/DrugSafetyChecker.tsx

No dose-range or interaction checking at the point of order

Prescriptions are free text: { name, dosage, frequency, duration } with no formulary link, so nothing can catch a paediatric overdose, a decimal slip (5 mg written as 50 mg), or a contraindicated pair. This is the single most common category of preventable harm in hospital medicine, and it is exactly the class of error software is good at catching.

src/lib/types.ts → Prescription.medications[]

A finalized clinical note can still be rewritten

Encounter.status has a Finalized state, but the update rule permits any staff member in the clinic to edit the note afterwards, silently and in place. A medical record must be append-only once signed: corrections are amendments that sit alongside the original with their own author and timestamp. As it stands, a note can be altered after an adverse event and the chart will show no trace — which makes the whole record hard to defend, not just that one entry.

firestore.rules → match /encounters

Vitals are stored as strings, so nothing can trend or alarm on them

Observation.value is a string. “120/80”, “120 / 80” and “bp 120 over 80” are three different values to this system and none of them is a number. That rules out sepsis and deterioration scoring (NEWS/MEWS), growth charts, and any “this reading is dangerous” alert — the things that make vitals worth recording electronically at all.

src/lib/types.ts → Observation.value: string

No patient-identity safeguards beyond one unique number

Registration checks the hospital number for duplicates and stops there. Real hospitals get the same patient registered three times under variant spellings, and then half the history is on the wrong chart. An EMR needs fuzzy duplicate detection at registration and a supervised record-merge tool. Merging is unglamorous and it is the difference between a chart you can trust and one you can’t.

No prescriber binding on a prescription

doctorName is a string copied onto the record. Nothing binds the order to a verified, licensed prescriber identity, and nothing stops the field being wrong. For controlled drugs that is a regulatory problem as much as a clinical one.

src/lib/types.ts → Prescription.doctorName

No backup, restore, or full-record export

I found no scheduled export, no restore procedure, and no way for a hospital to take its own data out. This is the question that ends procurement conversations: a medical director cannot put a hospital’s legal records somewhere they cannot retrieve them from, and cannot leave a vendor they cannot export from. It is also the only protection against a bad migration or a mistaken cascade delete.

Blocks the roadmap6 gaps

Architectural, and in the way

These aren’t safety issues, but each one prevents several features from being built at all — including ones already asked for.

Nothing runs unless a browser tab is open

There are no Cloud Functions and no scheduler anywhere in the project. Everything happens in a client. That means no automated appointment reminders, no overnight sync, no drug-expiry alerts, no month-end reports, no backup job — and it is why “automated WhatsApp reminders” cannot be built as asked today. This is the single highest-leverage thing to add, because at least six other features are waiting behind it.

No server-side validation of clinical data

Clients write to Firestore directly. Security rules check who and which tenant, but not what: an encounter with no SOAP body, a negative drug quantity, or a date of birth in 2190 is accepted. Rules can enforce shape, and the writes that matter clinically should go through a server route that validates them.

Roles are read from a document, not a token

Every rule evaluation does a get() on users/{uid} to learn the caller’s role. That costs a read per check, caps how complex rules can get, and — the real problem — means revoking someone’s access depends on a document write rather than a token that expires. Custom claims set server-side on signup and on any role change fix all three. isSuperAdmin already reads a claim this way; the rest should follow.

firestore.rules → userDoc()

The clinic API key is stored in three places, one of them widely readable

Key generation writes it to api_keys/{theKeyItself}, to clinics/{id}.apiConfig, and to users/{uid}.apiConfig. The clinic document has to stay broadly readable so a patient can pick a clinic to link their record to — so the secret has to move out of it, into a collection only the owning admin can read. Tightening the rule instead would break account linking.

src/app/dashboard/developers/page.tsx:66–80

Patient account linking is done client-side, on trust

Linking a portal account to a hospital record is a client write that sets patientId on the user’s own profile after the client checks a code. A crafted request can skip the check. This belongs in a server route that verifies the patient code against the surname and then writes the link — the same shape the staff-invite flow already uses.

src/app/dashboard/my-records/page.tsx

No consent record and no break-the-glass access

Two things every hospital deployment eventually needs: a record of what the patient consented to and when, and a way for a clinician to reach a chart they don’t normally have rights to in an emergency — while being made to state a reason that lands in the audit trail. Right now access is binary, so the emergency case forces either over-broad permissions or a clinician locked out at the worst moment.

Expected by any hospital buyer12 gaps

Procurement checklist

Nothing here is dangerous by its absence, but each is something a Nigerian tertiary hospital will ask about directly during evaluation. Roughly in the order they tend to come up.

Coded diagnoses (ICD-10 / SNOMED) — diagnosis is free text, which blocks claims, reporting and any epidemiology
NHIA / HMO claims workflow — pre-authorisation, capitation lists, claim batching and rejection handling
Lab analyser interfacing — results are typed by hand today; ASTM/HL7 links remove a transcription-error class
HL7 FHIR export — the lingua franca for referrals, HIEs and any government reporting
Imaging / PACS links — DICOM study references on the chart, even without an internal viewer
Discharge summaries & referral letters — generated documents, not free text pasted into a note
Antenatal & maternity module — a very large share of Nigerian hospital volume, with its own visit schedule
Theatre scheduling — lists, staffing, pre-op checklists, consent capture
Blood bank — group, crossmatch, unit tracking, transfusion reactions
Statutory registers — birth, death and notifiable-disease reporting are legal obligations, not features
Immunization schedules — immunizations[] is stored but no schedule logic or due/overdue view
Multi-site hospitals — one tenant, several branches, shared patients, separate stock and staffing

Compliance you should name before a customer does4 gaps

Do early, cheaply

NDPR obligations aren’t represented anywhere in the system

Nigeria’s Data Protection Regulation gives patients access and correction rights and imposes a breach-notification duty. None of it exists as a feature: no data-subject access export, no retention schedule, no record of what was disclosed to whom. These are cheap to build early and expensive to retrofit once there is real data.

No retention or archival policy

Records currently live forever, and the offline mirror keeps copies on every device that has ever synced. A retention schedule and a device-wipe-on-signout path are both needed before a hospital’s legal team asks.

The audit trail isn’t tamper-evident

It is now append-only by rule, which is the important half. The other half is making alteration detectable — hash chaining, or export to storage the application itself cannot rewrite.

No environment separation visible

One Firebase project appears to serve everything. Real patient data and a developer testing a cascade delete should not share a database. The emulator config added for the rules tests is a start.

If you only do three things

  1. Add a scheduler. One Cloud Function on a cron unblocks reminders, backups, expiry alerts and reports in one move. Nothing else on this page unlocks as much per hour spent.
  2. Put allergy and dose checking in the prescribing path. Not as a panel a clinician can open — as an interruption they have to dismiss with a reason. This is the gap most likely to hurt someone.
  3. Make finalized notes immutable, with amendments. Cheap to implement, and it is what turns the chart into a record a hospital can defend.

Coded diagnoses are the natural fourth: free-text diagnosis quietly blocks claims, reporting and interop all at once, and the longer it stays free text the more history has to be re-coded later.

Assessment based on a read of the repository at the current commit — security rules, the offline and sync layers, the dashboard routes and the AI flows. Deployment configuration, backup arrangements and any commercial or regulatory agreements were not in scope, so treat the compliance section as a prompt for your own review rather than a finding.
